Memory as a learned policy: a budgeted write problem

LLM agents that run for hours or days accumulate more context than any context window can hold. The store grows from use. The control layer that decides what to commit doesn’t. We turn that layer into a learned policy and frame the whole thing as a budgeted decision problem — with a label-free dense reward that stops the policy from cheating.

Parth Kocheta · University of Maryland Read the paper (PDF) Code on GitHub

The problem, in one paragraph

Every memory system shipping for LLM agents in 2026 — Mem0, Letta, Mastra, Zep, our own KGmem — pairs an external store (vector index, knowledge graph, or compressed log) with a hand-written control layer. The store grows from use: facts come in, get edited, get retrieved. The control layer is fixed at design time. A Mem0 prompt that misclassifies a fact today will misclassify the same fact in a year. The store is dynamic; the operating system over the store is not.

We ask whether that operating system can be learned from the same answer-correctness signal we already use to evaluate it.

Reframing: memory is a budgeted decision problem

Most prior work frames memory as retrieval-augmented generation: shovel everything into a vector store, retrieve top-k at read time, let the model do the rest. That works when storage is free. It doesn’t when storage is the bottleneck — which is the regime that matters as agents get long enough to accumulate hundreds of thousands of turns and tens of millions of tokens of context.

A cleaner formulation: assume a hard retention budget $K$. The write policy gets to keep at most $K$ items per conversation. Its job is to pick which $K$ matter. The read policy is a fixed stack (BM25 over entity text + dense embedding similarity + named-entity lookup, fused via reciprocal-rank fusion) that operates on whatever the write policy leaves behind. Performance is measured as the area under the efficiency curve as $K$ varies.

Why budget the writes, not the reads

An audit on 50 LoCoMo questions showed BM25 alone is the strongest single retriever at $0.62$ accuracy; dense was $0.42$, NER+dense was $0.44$. A learned read policy can’t meaningfully beat a hybrid BM25+dense+NER stack. So the read policy isn’t the ceiling. The write policy — which decides what BM25 gets to BM25 over — is.

The reward design problem (and why everyone’s answer is wrong)

Outcome supervision on memory writes is delayed and noisy: a write op only matters if the read policy can later use what was stored. Running a frozen reader against a held-out QA battery on every write trajectory works in principle but is slow and judge-noisy. Most training pipelines reach for a denser proxy.

The obvious proxy is evidence coverage: of the dialogue turns a held-out question depends on (LoCoMo annotates these as evidence dia_ids), what fraction did the policy preserve in memory? It’s deterministic, free to compute, and dense.

The problem: in a controlled study, evidence coverage correlates with the trivial number of turns kept feature at Spearman $\rho \approx 0.98$. Almost any policy that retains more turns will score higher under coverage, regardless of which turns it retains. A policy that wins coverage by 5 points might just be storing 5% more stuff.

Worse, evidence coverage requires per-question dia_id annotations, which research benchmarks ship and real personal-AI deployments do not. A user’s ChatGPT export does not come with “this answer required turn 47 from session 9.” If the only training signal we can build needs labels real users won’t produce, the technique is benchmark-shaped.

Reader-overlap, with a hard budget

The fix is two changes at once. First, replace evidence coverage with reader-overlap: for each held-out question, take what a frozen reader retrieves from the full conversation text — the set of dialogue turns the reader believes are relevant — and score how many of those turns the post-write knowledge graph preserved. No gold labels. The reader defines the target.

Second, enforce the budget as a hard cap. Before scoring, the post-write KG is pruned to at most $K$ entities (lowest-importance first). “Store everything verbatim” is structurally impossible, so the cost regulariser doesn’t need to fight that fight.

The reward equation in full 
reward(τ) = w_overlap · overlap(R, full_text, post_W_KG, Q)
          + w_qa      · mean_q judge(R(q, post_W_KG), gold_q)
          - λ · cost(ops)

overlap(R, F, M_τ, Q) = (1/|Q|) · Σ_q
                          |dia(R, F, q) ∩ dia(M_τ)| / |dia(R, F, q)|

where dia(R, F, q) = dia_ids of turns R retrieves from full-text F for q
      dia(M_τ)    = dia_ids reachable through provenance in budget-pruned M_τ

defaults: w_overlap = 0.7, w_qa = 0.3, λ = 0.001, K_max = 12

The reader is the same in both terms: a hand-coded composite of BM25 (over entity name + aliases + state values), dense embedding similarity, and named-entity lookup, fused via reciprocal-rank fusion. We hold it fixed during Phase B so write-side improvements aren’t entangled with read-side drift.

Why this works (and how we know)

A separate AI-researcher agent ran controlled experiments on the mempol problem. Three of its findings shaped this design.

1. Backend-retriever alignment is the dominant confound

On a paired 50-question LoCoMo sample with a frozen gpt-4o-mini reader, accuracy was $0.50$ on FlatBackend (BM25 + dense over windowed chunks) and $0.10$ on RichKGBackend (NER lookup only). McNemar $p = 1.9 \times 10^{-6}$. The KG backend lost on 20 questions Flat answered correctly, and won on zero. The fix — hybridising the KG retrieval with BM25 + dense via RRF — recovered $0.40$ of the $0.50$ ceiling. Backend-retriever alignment alone moves accuracy by 30+ points; if you don’t control for it, your write-policy study is mostly measuring this confound.

2. Reader-overlap correlates with gold coverage

On 1,404 simulated write trajectories (234 turns × 6 trajectories), reader-overlap and gold coverage track each other at global Spearman $\rho = 0.585$ and within-turn $\rho = 0.614$. The within-turn number is the GRPO-relevant one because GRPO ranks rollouts within a group rather than across episodes. A composite of reader-overlap and a lexical signal (weighted $\alpha = 0.55$ on overlap) tightens the within-episode ranking from $0.343$ to $0.374$.

Importantly, popular intrinsic-motivation rewards don’t work here. Random Network Distillation came in at $\rho = 0.082 \pm 0.041$ — statistically indistinguishable from zero. We’ve saved a research detour.

3. Strong retrievers mask write-policy quality

This is the most important finding for evaluation design. On LongMemEval-S, two heuristic write policies (BM25 vs Dense Reward) that differed materially in which turns they kept produced identical end-to-end QA accuracy (both $0.633$ at $F = 0.1$) because the downstream BM25+dense retrieval was strong enough to find the answer either way. Their retrieved top-10 overlapped at $7.4/10$ items.

Removing the retriever and feeding the kept turns directly to the reader exposed the underlying quality differences with high power: an Adaptive Reward policy beat BM25 at every retention fraction by $\geq 0.10$ recall (Wilcoxon $p = 3.1 \times 10^{-14}$). A two-step chain-of-thought prompt on the reader alone raised gpt-4o multi-session accuracy from $0.466$ to $0.737$.

Implication

End-to-end QA at full retention is a bad evaluation metric for write policies, because a strong retriever washes out the write differences. The right metrics are retriever-free evidence recall and QA at low retention, where the write decision matters most.

The success criterion

The discovery’s most useful contribution is the random-subsampling efficiency frontier. For each conversation and each retention fraction $F \in \{0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 1.0\}$, retain $K = \lceil F \cdot N \rceil$ uniformly-sampled turns and run the reader. The resulting accuracy-vs-$F$ curve on LoCoMo goes from $\sim 4\%$ at $F = 0.1$ to $\sim 56\%$ at $F = 0.9$. Any learned write policy must lie strictly above this curve at matched $F$, or the learning has bought nothing over chance.

We also report two clean negative controls. Topical Redundancy (retain turns most similar to other turns) collapses to evidence recall $\sim 0.01$ at $F = 0.20$. Zero-shot gpt-4o-mini as a write policy is statistically indistinguishable from random ($0.191$ vs $0.178$). If our learned policy ends up near either of these, training is broken.

Where this is heading

The current paper has a sharper claim than its earlier draft did. We are not claiming that a learned read policy beats hand-coded retrieval — the audit shows BM25 is hard to beat at retrieval. We are claiming that a learned write policy, given a hard retention budget and a label-free reader-overlap reward, can pick a better $K$-sized subset of a conversation than random subsampling or a BM25 heuristic can.

That smaller claim has three nice properties. It is falsifiable — either we beat random and BM25 at low $F$ or we don’t, and AUEC is a single number that closes the loop. It is deployment-shaped — real assistants face hard memory budgets, and retention is the actual bottleneck, not retrieval. And it is label-free — the reader-overlap signal works on any conversation, including data without per-question evidence annotations.

The store holds whatever the user chooses to give it. The strategy — what to keep, what to discard, what to revisit — lives in the weights and transfers between users without moving any of their data.

Open questions

  1. Per-conversation rather than per-turn episodes. Per-turn gives clean credit assignment but rules out write strategies that span turns (“defer until I see if it comes up again”). The natural extension is multi-turn episodes with discounted across-turn rewards, but credit assignment gets murkier.
  2. Implicit reward from the next user turn. Mode~D in the eval framework: in deployment, the user’s follow-up message is the reward signal. Did the agent’s previous-turn writes preserve the context the user’s reply depends on? If this works, evidence labels are no longer needed even at training time.
  3. Free-form storage. Right now the op vocabulary has a fixed schema (9 entity types, 5 transition types). A more expressive substrate is a key-value store with arbitrary keys and schemas the model invents on the fly. Bigger search space, more interesting research question, harder to bootstrap. Probably the paper-2 design.
